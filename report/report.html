1Motivation

Who has the problem. Golf is getting older and more physically diverse at the same time. The number of on-course golfers aged 65 and older nearly doubled between 2016 and 2025 (+97%, National Golf Foundation), and seniors are about 22% of golf's traditional participant base. Among U.S. adults 65 and older, about 48% have doctor-diagnosed arthritis (CDC/NCHS), and 12.1% of all U.S. adults have a mobility disability, meaning serious difficulty walking or climbing stairs (CDC). Add beginners, golfers recovering from surgery, and adaptive golfers who play from a wheelchair or a single-rider cart, and a large share of golfers share one question before every round: "Can we actually play this course?"

How they handle it today. Tee-time sites tell you when a course is open and what it costs, but nothing about how hard it is on a body. So golfers piece it together: course websites, scattered reviews ("hilly", "lots of water"), calls to the pro shop, and word of mouth. When I tried to research 15 public courses near New Haven this way, the public sources covered on average only 1 of the 8 accessibility facts that matter (terrain, walkability, cart rules near greens, adaptive carts, forced carries, water, bunkers), and the sources often disagreed. The closest thing to a directory, the National Alliance for Accessible Golf's search engine, depends on facilities listing themselves.

What it costs. The cost is mostly frustration and lost rounds rather than dollars. A wrong guess means a miserable or unsafe day: a 70-year-old with a bad knee facing a course with 400+ feet of climbing, or a beginner facing a 150-yard carry over water. The common workaround is to stop playing with the people who need the easier course. My own motivation is my dad, who still loves the game but has a bad knee. I wanted a tool that answers "which course near us, or on our trip, will he enjoy?" with facts instead of guesses.

I tested the plain-AI option directly. I asked the same model CaddieSync uses (gpt-6-luna), without CaddieSync's data, for three flat, easy-walking courses near New Haven with total feet of climbing and holes with water. Its answer (excerpt):

"I can’t give trustworthy feet of climbing or exact water-carry hole counts without course-specific GPS elevation data and a definition of “shots over water” (for example, whether a lateral water hazard counts). Those figures can vary by tees and walking route, so I don’t want to invent them. For a first pass, Alling Memorial Golf Course in New Haven is the clearest nearby option to investigate for an easier walk. I’…"

It is honest that it lacks the data, and its one suggestion, Alling Memorial, is exactly the kind of guess that goes wrong: CaddieSync's measurements show Alling has 382 feet of climbing and 9 of 18 holes with a stretch steeper than a wheelchair ramp.

What CaddieSync does differently.

  • It measures, instead of guessing or relying on self-reporting. An offline pipeline downloads every New England golf course from OpenStreetMap, where volunteers have drawn most courses hole by hole, and measures each hole against USGS elevation data: total climb, the steepest stretch compared with the 8.33% ADA limit for a wheelchair ramp, shots that must carry water, bunkers by each green, and the walk to the next tee. For the 15 courses I first researched by hand, measured facts rose from 1 to 5 of 8.
  • It searches by body, not by tee time. "My dad has a bad knee", "I use a SoloRider", "my wife is a beginner" become structured needs, and every course is scored for that group with reasons a golfer can check.
  • It is honest about gaps. Unknown facts lower a score instead of being guessed, and the app writes the exact questions to ask when you call.
  • It covers getting there and getting back. From mapped parking lots and clubhouses it measures the walk from the lot to the 1st tee (538 courses) and from the 9th green back to the clubhouse (517 courses), so a golfer knows whether stopping after nine is easy. It lists verified adaptive equipment (SoloRider carts, ParaGolfer chairs; 4 courses) and free PGA HOPE veterans programs (29 courses), all from published sources. Accessible parking and a step-free clubhouse are tagged on the map for only 17 courses, so for the rest the app says "Unknown: ask" and adds the questions to the call list instead of assuming ADA compliance.
  • It works for trips. Any town, region ("Cape Cod", "the Berkshires") or state; up to five courses, mapped, with booking links and directions.

Honest limits of the novelty: course rules (carts near greens, adaptive carts) are still mostly unknown, and tee-time availability is not included; CaddieSync links to each course's own booking page instead.

3The agents

CaddieSync uses three AI agents and five plain-code steps. The design rule is simple: the AI interprets and explains; code handles every fact, price, distance and safety decision. No AI output can set a course fact, because the AI's structured outputs have no field for one.

Figure 2. How the agents are organized. Lime boxes are AI agents (gpt-6-luna); white boxes are plain code; the dark box is the course database, built once offline with no AI.

The code steps between them are the safety and cost backbone: the input guard scans for attacks; the profile floor makes saved needs impossible to loosen; the Course Locator resolves the place (2,747 towns plus regions), filters by distance, and pre-scores courses from measured facts so only the top 10 reach the AI; the fact check removes claims about unknown or nonexistent fields and caps scores when key facts are missing; and the pick check drops any course the Concierge invents, then copies every displayed fact, price tier, distance and link from the database.

Model choice. All three agents use gpt-6-luna through Portkey. It is the only model allowed on the course key, and it fits the job: the tasks are short form-filling, rubric scoring and short writing, so I run it with low reasoning effort. At $0.10 per million input tokens ($0.01 cached) and $0.50 per million output tokens, it keeps a search under half a cent. Using one model with fixed instructions per agent also maximizes prompt caching.

One real run, step by step

The query: "Long weekend on Cape Cod with my wife. She's a beginner and we want easy, flat courses with a driving range to warm up and somewhere for lunch. 3 rounds." The app shows each step in a "How CaddieSync decided" panel; the screenshots below are from that run, with the cache empty.

  1. Input guard (code, 2 ms): cleaned 153 characters; no attack patterns.
  2. Profiler (AI, about 4 s): 3,961 input tokens (2,814 cached), 127 output. Output: near = "Cape Cod", trip = true, rounds = 3, wants = driving_range + food_drink, terrain = flat_only, forced_carries = avoid_all, shorter_course = true, skill = beginner.
  3. Course Locator (code, 2 ms): Cape Cod resolves to a region center with a 35-mile radius; 63 courses qualify; the top 10 by pre-score go to the AI (Holly Ridge first; it has a mapped driving range).
  4. Course Analyst (AI ×10, about 6 s in parallel): about 2,236 input tokens each (about 2,110 cached). Holly Ridge and Falcon score 100; Highland Links 90.
  5. Fact check (code): removed 2 claims about facts the database doesn't have.
  6. Concierge (AI, about 3 s): 3,863 input, 267 output; picks Holly Ridge, Falcon, and Harwich Port, and notes that only Holly Ridge has a mapped range.
  7. Pick check (code): every pick is a real scored course; facts, map pins and links are copied from the database. Total: $0.0029, 13.4 s.

One user costs about $0.014 a month in AI. At 10,000 users the whole service costs about $72 a month, or $0.0072 per user, and hosting, not AI, becomes the largest line. Pre-warming the cache for every course and the 50 most common needs combinations would be a one-time cost of about $11.94.

What I did to keep costs down

  1. Course Locator in code. Instead of asking the AI about every nearby course (about 51 on average), a rule-based pre-score from the measured facts sends only the top 9. The cost of a search stays flat whether the database has 15 courses or 851. Without it, and the steps below, a search would cost about $0.0249, about 7× more.
  2. Accessibility cache. Each course score is saved under course + needs. Repeat-type searches skip the Analyst entirely.
  3. Prompt caching. Each agent's long instructions are byte-identical on every call, and per-run data comes after them, so OpenAI bills about 71% of input tokens at the cached rate (one tenth the price).
  4. Low reasoning effort and small outputs. Short structured forms instead of long essays; the Concierge is skipped when there is nothing to pick.
  5. No AI at all for data. All 12,561 holes were measured once by code, at zero token cost.

The cache cut cost by about 92% and time by 3–4× with identical scores. It actually improves consistency: without it, the same course could score 80 one run and 85 the next. The Locator is the one trade-off: courses ranked 11th or lower by the rule-based pre-score are never checked by the AI. In the Cape Cod run, the pre-score's top two (Holly Ridge 106, Falcon 102) were also the Analyst's top two (100, 100), which suggests the shortlist keeps the best options, but it can miss a course whose strengths only show up in review text.

5Safety

CaddieSync holds health-related information (bad knees, wheelchairs) and runs on a paid API key, so I designed for three kinds of risk. Every defense below is tested; results are in output/attack_results.json and tests/attack_test.py.

Careful estimate of time saved. Today, checking three candidate courses for a golfer with a bad knee means reading each website and reviews and calling the pro shop: roughly 10–15 minutes per course, or 30–45 minutes, and the answer is still mostly guesswork. The bigger impact is not minutes but rounds: a group that can find a course everyone can enjoy keeps playing together.

7Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • Course rules are mostly unknown. Cart access near greens and adaptive carts aren't on any map, so most scores are capped and come with "call to confirm". Adaptive equipment is verified for only 4 courses, and ADA parking and clubhouse access for 17; the walks are straight-line distances from the middle of a mapped lot.
  • Public vs. private is unknown for 91% of courses. Known private clubs are excluded; the rest show "Access: check" and the call list asks first.
  • No live tee times. There is no public tee-time API for these courses, and scraping booking sites breaks their terms; CaddieSync links to each course's own booking page. Price tiers are researched for only 12 featured facilities.
  • Map quality varies. 43 courses are outlines only; bunker depth isn't measured; water is detected only where mapped; elevation uses six points per hole from a 10-meter dataset, so very short steep pitches can be missed or exaggerated.
  • AI scores vary a little. The same course can score 80 one run and 85 the next, or 30 vs. 45 when the Profiler reads "knee surgery" slightly differently. The cache hides this for repeat questions, but it is real.
  • Free hosting. The live site sleeps after inactivity (about a 30-second first load), and accounts and the daily spending tally reset when Render restarts the app. Profiles survive in each user's browser copy.
  • Ground truth. I checked the measurements against known courses (coastal Short Beach is flat; Fox Hopyard and The VUE are hilly, matching reviews), but not against on-site surveys.

What I would build with another month

  1. Course-confirmed facts. A short form for pro shops (cart rules, adaptive carts, flags) with a "verified" badge, seeded by the app's call lists; partner with the National Alliance for Accessible Golf to merge listings.
  2. Golfer feedback loop. After a round, one question ("Was it as easy as we said?"), used to correct scores and learn which needs matter most.
  3. Real tee times through a booking partner API, and real price tiers for every course.
  4. National coverage. The pipeline is state-agnostic; the U.S. is an overnight run. Plus Postgres for durable accounts.
  5. A pilot with an adaptive golf program to measure rounds played and repeat use, the impact numbers that matter most.
